SAGE · Instructor-regulated access control

Baseline evaluation plan

What to run, against which published systems, in what order, and what breaks if a piece is skipped. The comparison is made by holding the tutor fixed and swapping only the policy.

Drafted 16 Sep 2026 Scope: full gatekeeper — RBAC + ABAC + CAC Status: none of these runs executed yet
Step 1

What is being claimed

Three claims, each with one experiment that can falsify it. Everything else in this plan exists to serve one of them. A result that supports none of these does not belong in the paper.

C1

Personalized control beats static control at equal helpfulness

The full gatekeeper withholds more of what should be withheld without withholding more of what should be given. Not "it refuses more" — anything can refuse more.

Falsified if a static policy reaches the same leakage rate at the same legitimate-help rate. Tested by the frontier below.

C2

Metacognition adds something mastery alone does not

Mastery-gated curriculum locks are decades old. The contribution is the layer above: distance, help-seeking, load, frustration, overconfidence.

Falsified if P3 (mastery gate) equals P4 (full CAC) on every axis. This is the single most attackable claim in the paper.

C3

Existing policy languages cannot express the predicate

A production, formally verified authorization language cannot state "withhold unless mastery on the overconfident ancestor topic is ≥ 0.80" without calling out to the learner model.

Falsified by writing it in Cedar. Cheapest result here, and the one that justifies a new model rather than a new ruleset.

Step 2 · Serves C1 and C2

One tutor, one corpus, one model, one question set. Only the policy layer changes, so any difference is attributable to policy rather than to retrieval or generation. P2 is a reimplementation of a published system's decision rule, not a strawman of our own invention.

Why over-blocking is P1 and learning outcome is P3. Over-blocking is cheap, needs no students, and its absence is the most likely reason a reviewer rejects a containment result. A classroom outcome study is the strongest evidence available and cannot be produced on a paper deadline; plan it as future work rather than as a blocking dependency.

Step 4

The headline figure

A bar chart of leakage rates invites the obvious objection: any policy wins that axis by refusing everything. Plotting both axes at once removes the objection, because both degenerate policies are on the chart and neither is attractive.

Sweeping your own thresholds (θ, the load and frustration cut-offs, the horizon) turns P4 from a point into a curve, which is a stronger claim than a single configuration: the frontier itself moves.

Second headline output: the capability matrix — one row per published system, columns for subject attributes, object attributes, state-dependence, graded disclosure, enforcement mechanism and adversary. The empty columns are the contribution.

Step 6

Order of execution

Ordered by result-per-hour, not by logical tidiness. The first three need no LLM calls and no students.

  1. Cedar expressiveness test → C3Write the static policy in Cedar, then try to write the CAC predicate. Self-contained, a day, and it converts "we named a thing" into "a verified language cannot say this".
  2. Build the legitimate question setBlocks every over-blocking number. Draw from logged in-region turns so it is not authored to flatter the policy.
  3. Replay P0–P4 offline → C1, C2The core table and the frontier plot. Uses shadow-mode decisions already being logged, so no student is affected.
  4. Signal ablations P4− → C2Same harness, one switch at a time. Turns one bundled result into five attributable ones.
  5. Disclosure complianceLive generation runs. Answers "is the cap real or advisory?", which a reviewer will ask the moment they read that it is a prompt instruction.
  6. Prompt-only baseline P5Most expensive, because every configuration needs fresh generations. Do it once the cheap results are banked.
  7. Classroom outcome studyFuture work. Name it as future work in the paper rather than letting a reviewer name it for you.
Step 7

Threats to validity, stated before a reviewer states them

  • Different corpora. Published comparators run on healthcare and enterprise data. Only their policy models transfer to this tutor, never their numbers. Say this explicitly; comparing absolute figures across corpora would be wrong.
  • A weak published bar. Chen et al. is a three-page proof of concept with two access levels and 40 synthetic profiles, and says so itself. Beating it is not the claim; being equivalent to it at the static layer, and adding a layer above, is.
  • Different adversary. SecureRAG defends against an untrusted server; this work assumes a trusted server and defends against an authorized-but-unready user. The two compose rather than compete, and claiming to beat it on security would be a category error.
  • Judge reliability. Leakage and over-blocking labels from an LLM judge need a human-agreement check on a sample, or the headline numbers rest on an unvalidated instrument.
  • Enforcement is off by default. Everything shipped runs in shadow mode. Replay results describe what the policy would do; say so, and keep it distinct from deployed effect.
  • Synthetic learner states. Load and frustration currently fire for no real student in the corpus. That is a property of the data, and simulation is how the mechanism is shown to work — but it must be labelled as simulation.
